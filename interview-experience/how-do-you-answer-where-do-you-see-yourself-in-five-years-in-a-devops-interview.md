# How do you answer 'where do you see yourself in five years' in a DevOps interview?

**Short answer:** Give an honest direction that fits the role you are interviewing for, a reason for it, and evidence you are already moving that way. For most DevOps candidates that means one of three tracks - **deep technical** (senior, staff or principal engineer), **architecture** (platform or solutions architect), or **people leadership** (team lead, engineering manager) - and saying clearly that you want to stay hands-on for the next couple of years. Finish by asking how people in the team have grown.

## Detail

**Why it is asked.** The interviewer wants to know three things: will you stay long enough to be worth the investment, does your ambition fit what the role can offer, and do you have any self-awareness about your own development. It is not a test of whether you have a detailed plan - nobody's five-year plan survives - but whether you have a direction.

**The three tracks, and how to talk about each.**

- **Technical depth (IC track).** "I want to be the person the team goes to for Kubernetes and delivery platforms - senior and then staff level, owning cross-team technical decisions." Safe for almost any engineering role.
- **Architecture.** "Platform or solutions architect, designing systems across teams." Add, and mean, that good architects stay hands-on - building proofs of concept, reviewing infrastructure code, being on the escalation path for hard problems. An architect who no longer touches systems designs things that do not work, and interviewers know it.
- **People leadership.** "Leading a platform team." Say what draws you to it (mentoring, unblocking others) and what you already do (onboarding new joiners, running retros). Be careful in a role explicitly hired for hands-on work - pair it with "after a few years of building."

**Show the path, not just the destination.** A credible answer names steps already in motion: writing design documents and architecture decision records, leading a cross-team initiative, mentoring a junior engineer, running a platform review, or learning a domain the role needs. Certifications are fine as a supplement, but they are not a career plan on their own.

**Fit the answer to the role.** If you are interviewing for a hands-on platform engineer role and say "in five years I want to manage people and stop coding", you have told them the role is a stepping stone. If you are interviewing for a team lead role and say "I never want to manage anyone", you have also mis-fitted. Read the job description and match the direction to what the company can genuinely offer.

**AI and the changing role.** It is reasonable to mention adapting to AI-assisted tooling - using assistants and agents well, with proper guardrails - as part of staying current. Keep it concrete rather than speculative.

**What to avoid.**

- "In your job" - a cliché that reads as glib.
- "I don't know" or "wherever the company needs me" - sounds directionless.
- Plans that leave the field ("my own startup", "an MBA and then product") in a role that needs long-term commitment.
- A detailed title-and-salary timeline, which sounds transactional.

## Example

```text
THREE ADAPTABLE ANSWERS — pick the one that is true for you

  Technical depth
    "For the next few years I want to go deep on platform engineering -
     EKS, Terraform, delivery pipelines - and grow into a staff-level role
     where I own cross-team technical decisions. I'm already leading our
     cluster-upgrade automation and mentoring a junior engineer."

  Architecture
    "Two or three years hands-on, then a platform or solutions architect.
     I want to stay close to the technology even then - prototypes,
     reviewing IaC, on the escalation path. I've started by writing the
     design doc and ADRs for our multi-region work."

  People leadership
    "Building for a few more years, then leading a platform team. I enjoy
     unblocking people: I run onboarding for new joiners and our
     incident retros. I'd want to stay technical enough to review designs."

  Close with a question
    "How have senior engineers on this team grown over the last few years?"
```

## Interview tips

- Keep it to about 45-60 seconds. It is a short question and a long answer reads as rehearsed.
- Expect the follow-up "what are you doing about it now?" - have two concrete actions ready.
- The common weak answer is a vague "architect in five years" with no reason and nothing already in motion. A destination without evidence sounds like a title you have read about.
- If you want management, show you understand it is a different job, not a promotion for being the best engineer.
- Asking how others on the team have grown turns the question into a conversation and gives you real information about the role.

**Telling it as a STAR story** - an adaptable example, labelled as such; use your own facts:

- **Situation:** "In a senior DevOps interview I was asked whether I saw myself staying hands-on, moving into management, or into architecture in five years."
- **Task:** "I wanted an honest answer that showed ambition but still fitted a hands-on role."
- **Action:** "I said that for the next two or three years I want to stay deeply hands-on, owning EKS, Terraform and CI/CD and getting good at using AI tooling safely in that work, because good architecture comes from having operated systems yourself. Longer term I see myself as a platform architect rather than a people manager. I gave the evidence: I wrote the design doc and ADRs for our multi-region migration and run the team's architecture reviews. I said I'd still build prototypes and review pull requests as an architect, then asked how their senior engineers had grown."
- **Result:** "The interviewer said that matched how their architects work, and the conversation moved on to the technical leadership expected in the role - ground I was comfortable on."
